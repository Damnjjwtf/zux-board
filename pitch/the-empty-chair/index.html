A JJ Growth Idea for Culinary Backstreets

The Empty Chair

On the dope board, Virgin Voyages' “Jen AI” (2023) let customers generate a personalized video invite from Jennifer Lopez and send it to their crew — 25,000+ invites made. The invite was the ad: a personal, sendable thing. JJ's question: could Culinary Backstreets hand every booker something worth sending — a coupon built to be shared?

The idea in one line

Every booking comes with one seat to give away.

How it works

Three beats. A booking becomes a gift, a gift becomes a booking, and an empty seat does the recruiting.

1

Book

The confirmation for every walk carries one personal guest pass — a unique, single-use code that takes a set amount off one seat, on any walk, in any Culinary Backstreets city.

2

Share

The pass travels as an invitation, not a coupon: a small card that names the giver, their walk, and their master. The discount rides inside a gift. People forward invitations; they feel cheap forwarding coupons.

Both sides win

The friend books with the pass. The giver earns credit toward their next walk. Culinary Backstreets fills a seat on a walk that was already running — and meets a new customer who arrived on a friend's word, not an ad.

Why the math works

A walk that runs has already paid for the guide and the route. The ninth and tenth seats cost almost nothing to fill, and an empty seat earns exactly zero. A discounted seat sold beats a full-price seat empty — and the customer it brings was acquired for the discount alone, with no ad spend in front of it.

Bulletproof

The rules that make it safe today. Six of them, and the idea doesn't run without all six.

1

Unique codes only

Every pass is minted for one booking and dies on use or expiry. There is no public code — nothing for coupon sites to scrape, nothing to train strangers to wait for a sale.

2

It unlocks after the walk

The pass activates once the giver's walk has happened (or the cancellation window has closed), so nobody can book, share, and refund their way to a free discount.

3

New customers only

The friend's booking is matched on email and payment details; the giver can't spend their own pass, and one pass can never stack with another offer.

4

Ninety days, then it lapses

A pass is a nudge with a deadline, not a liability that lives forever. At any moment, CB can count exactly how many live passes exist and what they could cost, worst case.

5

Real seats only

A pass books into real inventory like any other seat — it can never overfill a walk, and if a date is full it simply holds for another.

6

Capped pilot

Launch in one city, with a hard cap on passes minted (say the first 500), and read the numbers before scaling: passes given, passes used, cost per new customer next to the cost of buying that customer with ads.

Two prompts, never a drip

The pass is offered exactly twice — at the two moments it feels like a gift instead of a sales tactic.

First — in the booking confirmation

“Know someone who'd love this walk?”

The pass arrives with the good news that the trip is real. Sharing it is part of the excitement of booking, not a chore added later.

Second — after the walk, at peak delight

“You met Hüsam Hoca. Who else needs to?”

The one moment the giver's recommendation carries its full weight: they have just stood where the card says they stood.

No discount newsletter, no reminder blasts — the moment a brand trains people to wait for coupons, full price starts to look like a tax on the loyal.

The AI's small job

The card the giver sends can be generated at booking from real booking data — the giver's name, the walk, the master, the date. No fake Jen, no licensed likeness, no synthetic person. The celebrity in the friend's inbox is the giver. AI does the personalizing; the trust is entirely human.

The offer

The numbers are CB's to set

The structure above is the idea. The discount size, the giver's credit, and the pilot city are Culinary Backstreets' call — sized to their margins, tested small, scaled only if the loop pays for itself.
